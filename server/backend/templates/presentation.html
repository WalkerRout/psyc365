The Human Brain Reactivates Context-Specific Past Information at Event Boundaries of Naturalistic Experiences

Hahamy, A., Dubossarsky, H., & Behrens, T. E. J. (2023). The human brain reactivates context-specific past information at event boundaries of naturalistic experiences. Nature Neuroscience, 26(6), 1080-1089. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-023-01331-6

Rationale

The Big Picture

We consciously experience the world as a continuous stream, but remember it in chunks (discrete slices with beginnings and endings)... how does the brain wire these chunks together into a coherent understanding of what is actively happening?

What We Already Know

Existing research has established:

  • The brain breaks continuous experience into discrete "events" at moments of change. Similar to a scene transition in a movie, or a shift in topic during a conversation. These transition points are referred to as event boundaries (Zacks et al., 2007; Radvansky & Zacks, 2011).
  • The hippocampus plays a central role in a) detecting these boundaries and b) encoding each event into memory (Ben-Yakov et al., 2013; Ben-Yakov & Henson, 2018; Zheng et al., 2022).
  • During sleep/rest, the brain can replay neural patterns from past experiences to help consolidate memories (Foster, 2017; Findlay et al., 2020). In rodents, this replay has been shown to piece together sequential events, as if the brain is building a mental map of its experiences (Gupta et al., 2010; Pfeiffer & Foster, 2013; Karlsson & Frank, 2009).
  • In humans, similar replay-like activity (what we refer to as reinstatement in class, and reactivation by the authors) has been observed during offline periods, and to a certain extent, right after an event ends (Sols et al., 2017; Silva et al., 2019; Tambini & Davachi, 2019).

The Knowledge Gap

While we can say we know that the brain replays memories during downtime (Foster, 2017; Findlay et al., 2020), no one had shown that the brain selectively reactivates relevant past experiences in realtime during some ongoing experience. Specifically, it was unknown whether the brain, at the exact moment a scene changes, retrieves not just that specific scene, but also older, contextually related memories that help make sense of what is actively happening.

Put in naturalistic terms: does your brain, at every scene change in a movie, automatically pull up earlier scenes that are relevant to understanding the current plot? Furthermore, does it do achieve this using the hippocampus and default mode network, the same regions involved in memory and narrative comprehension (Yeshurun et al., 2021)?

Motivation and Questions

We know the brain replays memories offline and segments experience at event boundaries, but nobody had connected these two ideas during live, ongoing experience...

Research Question

When the brain encounters an event boundary during a naturalistic experience, does it reactivate neural representations of distant, yet contextually relevant, past events alongside the event that just ended?

Hypotheses

The authors approached this question by breaking it down into three testable predictions:

  1. Reactivation of remote past events occurs at event boundaries: At event boundaries, brain activity patterns will resemble patterns from earlier scenes more than patterns from upcoming scenes. This is indicative that the brain is replaying past information, not just propagating the active signal.
  2. Reactivation is specific to event boundaries: The effect should be stronger at event boundaries than at other, non-boundary timepoints. This helps rule out the possibility that the brain is constantly replaying past events, regardless of what is happening.
  3. Reactivation is selective for relevant past events: Past events that are semantically related to the current narrative context should be reactivated more strongly than unrelated past events. Again, this suggests that the brain is not replaying everything, but searching for context to aid comprehension of the active situation.

Why These Hypotheses?

Prior work showed offline replay exists (Foster, 2017) and that the just-ended event is briefly reinstated at boundaries (Sols et al., 2017; Silva et al., 2019). The logical next step is to question whether remote events, that is ones that happened minutes ago, not seconds, are selectively brought back online at boundaries based on their relevance to the current moment. This would reframe reactivation from a simple repeat of the recent past into an active mechanism for supporting narrative understanding in realtime.

Research Design and Procedure

Design Overview

This is a within-subjects, correlational neuroimaging study using multimodal, naturalistic stimuli. There is no experimental manipulation in the traditional sense; participants simply watched a movie or listened to a story while being scanned. The "manipulation" is hidden in/provided by the stimulus itself, the narrative naturally contains event boundaries and scenes of varying semantic relatedness, which the authors exploit analytically.

Participants and Task

The study analyzed two existing fMRI datasets. In both, healthy adult participants passively experienced a continuous narrative while undergoing a whole-brain BOLD fMRI scan:

Why These Participants and Stimuli?

The authors deliberately chose naturalistic stimuli (a movie and a story) over traditional lab tasks because naturalistic narratives contain unique, non-repeating events. Unlike typical replay experiments that repeat the same trials over and over, naturalistic stimuli makes it possible to test whether the brain reactivates specific past events based on their relevance, rather than just replaying an overtrained sequence. Using two different datasets (one visual, one auditory) also allows the authors to examine whether the effect generalizes across modalities.

How the Design Addresses the Research Questions

The key analytic tool is representational similarity analysis (RSA) (Kriegeskorte et al., 2008). The researchers extracted the BOLD activation pattern (the spatial pattern of activity across voxels) at every event boundary and during every scene. They then asked, "does the pattern at a given boundary look more like the patterns from either past scenes or future scenes"?

Independent Variables

  • Timepoint type (boundary vs non-boundary): Whether a given fMRI timepoint falls at an event boundary or at a control timepoint adjacent to the boundary. This was determined by scene transition timings defined by independent raters, shifted by 6 seconds (4 TRs) to account for BOLD response' hemodynamic delay (the ~5-6 second lag between neural activity and blood flow change, as mentioned in class). In the authors' words: "We defined event boundaries as TRs in which scenes transitioned (TR = 1.5s)."
  • Semantic relatedness of past scenes: Measured using bag-of-words, each scene is represented by effectively counting the words that occur in it. Similarity between scenes is then simply computed by the overlap in their word content. This is not manipulated, but varies naturally across the narrative.

Dependent Variables

  • Reactivation index: The key measure, a difference between the average similarity of boundary patterns to past scene patterns versus future scene patterns. A positive value means the brain at a boundary looks more like where it has been (the past) than where it is going (the future), which is taken to be evidence of reactivation. Again, this is measured from the BOLD signal using RSA on multivoxel activation patterns (as discussed numerous times in class).

Results and Findings

Remote Past Events are Reactivated at Boundaries

At event boundaries, brain activity patterns in the posterior hippocampus, precuneus/retrosplenial cortex, and angular gyrus/lateral occipital cortex were significantly more similar to patterns from past scenes than future scenes (positive reactivation index). This was true in both datasets individually and in a pooled mega-analysis.

This is direct support for the authors' first hypothesis. The brain is not just passively transitioning between scenes; it is actively replaying representations of events that happened minutes earlier, in regions known for episodic memory (hippocampus) and narrative comprehension (default mode network; Chang et al., 2021; Yeshurun et al., 2021).

Reactivation is Specific to Event Boundaries

When the reactivation index at event boundaries was compared against control timepoints (moments adjacent to boundaries), the same three regions showed significantly stronger reactivation at boundaries specifically. Reactivation timecourses peaked around 6 to 9 seconds after boundaries, consistent with the BOLD hemodynamic response timing.

Cross-validation across datasets confirmed the effect: regions defined from one dataset showed significant reactivation in the other (meta-analysis via Fisher's test: P values are not mentioned on this page, they are available in the original paper for those interested).

This directly supports the second hypothesis. The brain is not constantly replaying the past; it specifically does so at targeted moments when the narrative context shifts.

Reactivation Preferentially Targets Relevant Past Events

Using the bag-of-words algorithm to measure semantic similarity between scenes, the authors found that past scenes sharing more words with the current context were more strongly reactivated. This selection of context was most robust in the precuneus/retrosplenial cortex, where it replicated across both datasets and analysis methods.

Following the pattern, this supports the third hypothesis, and it is (probably) the most important finding. The brain is not replaying all past events equally. It is selectively retrieving the ones that are most relevant to understanding the active moment, as if polling its own history for useful context.

Reactivation is Shared Across People

Between-participant analyses (correlating one person's scene patterns with another person's boundary patterns) revealed the same three regions, demonstrating that reactivated representations are not just voxel-specific quirks of one individual brain; they have shared structure between individuals (Chen et al., 2017; Zadbood et al., 2017).

Discussion

Interpretation of Findings

The authors propose that event boundaries serve as natural triggers for memory reactivation. At these moments, the hippocampus and default mode network work together to retrieve past event representations that are relevant to the current situation, binding them with the ongoing experience. The process is an active, selective retrieval of specific past events based on their contextual relevance and not passive carry-over of recent activity.

They frame this as a constructive mechanism, in that the brain builds its understanding of an unfolding narrative by continuously embedding relevant pieces of past experience into the present. The precuneus/retrosplenial cortex appears to select which past events to reactivate (based on semantic context), while the hippocampus may bind those reactivated events with the current event model to form some sort of interconnected representation (Eichenbaum, 2000; Shohamy & Wagner, 2008).

Implications for the Field

  • Reactivation goes beyond memory consolidation. Previous work framed replay/reactivation primarily as a mechanism for strengthening memories during sleep or rest (Foster, 2017; Tambini & Davachi, 2019). This study shows reactivation also operates online, during active experience, suggesting it plays a broader role than just consolidation. It is a tool to help us understand the present, not just remember the past.
  • The default mode network has an online function. The DMN, which we discussed in class as typically being suppressed during attention-demanding tasks, is traditionally viewed as an "offline" network, active during rest and mindwandering (Buckner et al., 2008). These findings contest that idea, and support the growing body of work suggesting that the DMN seems to play an active role during naturalistic experience (Chang et al., 2021; Yeshurun et al., 2021).
  • Most prior replay studies used highly controlled tasks with repeated trials, meaning replay reflected an overtrained schema rather than specific event memories (Pfeiffer, 2020). By using naturalistic stimuli where every event is unique, this study shows the brain can selectively reactivate individual past events, which is a qualitatively different kind of computation.

External Relevance

At the most basic level, this study addresses how we make sense of what is happening to us. Every time you watch a movie, read a book, or follow a conversation, your brain is doing the work described here; pulling up relevant memories at transition points to help you understand the narrative. This is something we do effortlessly and constantly, and now we have a candidate neural mechanism for it.

For researchers, this opens up questions about what happens when this mechanism breaks down. Conditions that involve fragmented narrative understanding, such as Alzheimer's disease or certain forms of amnesia, could involve disruptions to boundary-triggered reactivation (Buckner et al., 2008). Understanding the mechanism in healthy brains is a first step toward understanding what goes wrong in clinical populations.

Critique

Strengths

  • Naturalistic stimuli let the authors study reactivation of unique, one-time events rather than overtrained repeated trials. RSA was the right analytic tool for this, as it can compare multivoxel patterns across specific timepoints without requiring repeated conditions, making it well-suited to continuous, non-repeating narratives where those traditional contrast-based fMRI approaches would not apply.
  • Replication across two independent datasets with different modalities (movie vs story), different narrative structures (single plot vs. alternating storylines), and different participant groups. This cross-dataset validation, combined with the mega-analysis, is rigorous and substantially strengthens the generalizability of the findings.
  • The past-minus-future reactivation index is a methodologically viable control. It cancels out symmetric scene-to-scene similarity, isolating genuine reactivation from pattern overlap between neighbouring scenes. The boundary specificity analysis (comparing boundaries to adjacent control timepoints) and the flushing control (excluding post-boundary events) further rule out confounders.

Limitations

  • This study is correlational only. Reactivation occurs at boundaries, but the data cannot establish that it is causally required for comprehension.
  • BOLDs ~5-6 second lag hurts temporal precision. The authors compensate with a 4-TR shift, which is standard practice, but it still means that the exact moment of reactivation relative to the boundary is blurry. The authors acknowledge this directly, and it is why they prefer/use the term "reactivation" over "replay."
  • The bag-of-words algorithm used to measure semantic relatedness is dead simple, it captures word overlap but not any deeper semantic or narrative meaning. A more sophisticated approach (such as sentence embeddings) might reveal stronger/different patterns of context selectivity.
  • Context-selective reactivation replicated across datasets only in the precuneus/retrosplenial cortex. The hippocampus and angular gyrus showed reactivation but not clear content selectivity, limiting how broadly the "selective" claim can be applied.
  • There is no behavioural measure. The study never tests whether stronger reactivation leads to better memory or understanding of the narrative. This is the most significant gap; without it, the functional significance of reactivation remains an inference.
  • Sample sizes (17 and 25) are modest. While the cross-dataset replication compensates for this to some extent, larger samples would provide more statistical power, particularly for the context-selectivity analysis (which showed weaker effects in the 21st Year dataset).
  • No predictive coding found. The authors expected to find boundary patterns resembling future events, but did not...

Do Conclusions Follow from Findings?

The three core findings of reactivation at boundaries, boundary specificity, and context selectivity in the precuneus, are each supported by appropriate analyses, controls, and cross-dataset replication. The data does support these claims. The broader conclusion, that reactivation is a "constructive mechanism" for building narrative understanding in real time, is a reasonable interpretation but goes beyond what the data strictly demonstrate. Without a behavioural measure linking reactivation to comprehension or memory, the claim that this process underlies understanding (rather than merely accompanying it) remains an inference. The authors are rather transparent about this; they frame it as a proposal, not a proven fact.

Future Directions

  • Link reactivation to behaviour; does stronger boundary reactivation predict better recall or comprehension?
  • More precise timing; researches could combine fMRI with EEG/MEG for granular reactivation timing.
  • Clinical populations; test whether disrupted boundary reactivation relates to fragmented memory in Alzheimers or PTSD.
  • It could also be interesting to explore narratives with unpredictable structure to probe the interaction between prediction and reactivation.

Take-Home Conclusion

When a scene shifts, your brain doesn't immediately move on. It replays relevant earlier moments to help you make sense of what is happening now. This study provides initial evidence that this happens selectively, in real time, at event boundaries, in the hippocampus and default mode network.
